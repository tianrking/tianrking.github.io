Across two Thailand trips, these cities offer very different ways to read the past. In Bangkok, dynastic history still occupies a visible part of the living capital. Ayutthaya’s brick towers and foundations sketch the shape of a former capital. Chiang Mai’s old town belongs to the distinct history of Lanna. All three have temples, but the temples explain different things.

## Bangkok: the river and the royal city {/* #heritage */}

Begin with the Chao Phraya. The Rattanakosin royal center lies on the eastern bank, with the Grand Palace as an anchor. Landings, waterways and trading neighborhoods show another side of Bangkok: the city worked through connections between its banks, not only through the palace precinct. On a map, markets and piers tell as much about their surroundings as the rooflines of the temples. [Tourism Authority of Thailand: The Grand Palace](https://www.tourismthailand.org/Attraction/the-grand-palace)

A location beside the river, or the relationship between a tower and its courtyard, makes a useful clue when identifying an older photograph. A golden roof on its own rarely tells you which temple it is.

## Ayutthaya: the scale of a ruined capital {/* #ayutthaya */}

Founded in the mid-fourteenth century, Ayutthaya became a capital of Siam and fell to Burmese forces in the eighteenth century. Today the gaps between towers, the lines of precinct walls and the dimensions of monastery platforms are as informative as close-ups of their masonry. Put a photograph of one tower beside a wider view and it becomes easier to picture a whole city. [UNESCO: Historic City of Ayutthaya](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/576/)

A row of stupas makes Wat Phra Si Sanphet a place to compare; a Buddha head held by tree roots points toward Wat Mahathat. These are clues for identifying photographs, not a list of admissions or visits.

## Chiang Mai: a Lanna city still in use {/* #chiang-mai */}

Where Ayutthaya asks you to recover the outline of lost buildings, Chiang Mai puts old masonry beside active religious spaces. Wat Chedi Luang is an example: its monumental stupa and the temple buildings around it offer two different scales of religious life in the same precinct. [Tourism Authority of Thailand: Wat Chedi Luang](https://www.tourismthailand.org/Attraction/wat-chedi-luang-varavihara)

Sukhothai, Si Satchanalai and Kamphaeng Phet belong to another group of historic towns. They are not added to the two trips on the strength of an earlier planning discussion. [UNESCO: Sukhothai and associated historic towns](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/574/)

## Kanchanaburi: a nearer history of war {/* #kanchanaburi */}

Kanchanaburi belongs to a different historical period. The Burma–Thailand Railway is remembered not just through a bridge, but through the suffering and deaths of Allied prisoners of war and Asian laborers subjected to forced labor. Cemeteries, museums and the river each hold a different kind of evidence. The actual sites seen on the trip can be added when tickets or photographs identify them. [Australian Department of Veterans’ Affairs: Remembering the Burma–Thailand Railway](https://anzacportal.dva.gov.au/wars-and-missions/ww2/pows/burma-thailand-railway/remembering)
