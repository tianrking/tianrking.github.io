Tokyo makes sense at the scale of a street: a station exit, a bridge, a shopping arcade or a low row of buildings in the shadow of taller neighbors. Mount Fuji asks the opposite question. The outline of the mountain is recognizable, but the nearby street, field or water tells you where the viewer is standing. Putting these two scales together makes room for both city life and landscape.

## Tokyo: looking for Edo in the street plan {/* #heritage */}

At Nihonbashi, bridge, waterfront and commerce were intertwined in the Edo period. An old illustrated scroll can bring that relationship into focus when compared with the modern map. Asakusa offers another line of inquiry: a temple district around Sensō-ji, followed by the growth of nearby theater and entertainment streets. Neither place is listed here as a personal stop. [GO TOKYO: Nihonbashi in an Edo scroll](https://www.gotokyo.org/en/see-and-do/nature/waterfront/kidai-shoran-nihonbashi.html) · [GO TOKYO: Asakusa and Edo culture](https://www.gotokyo.org/en/story/walks-and-tours/edo_asakusa/index.html)

A few photographs of platform signs, crossings or storefronts can reconstruct a small real walking route. It matters less how many neighborhoods appear on a list than whether the details belong to the same walk.

## Mount Fuji: the viewpoint is part of the story {/* #mount-fuji */}

Mount Fuji is a volcano and a place repeatedly represented in religious practice and art. UNESCO inscribed it as “Fujisan, sacred place and source of artistic inspiration”; ascent routes, shrines and views from the foothills offer different ways to read the mountain. [UNESCO: Fujisan](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1418/)

The view may have been from a shore, a street or higher ground; the outline does not tell us which. A shoreline, a shop sign or a station name is a better clue. Once the viewpoint is known, the text can describe a real relationship between mountain and town or water instead of borrowing someone else’s famous vantage point.
